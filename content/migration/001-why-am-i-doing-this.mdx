For about eight years my job has been keeping other people's software alive. Kubernetes, Terraform,
pipelines, dashboards, alerts, postmortems. I'm comfortable there. I know what a 3am page feels like
and I know which parts of a distributed system lie to you first.

Then, over the last year, roughly every system I was asked to run started containing a model.

Not in an interesting way, at first. A team would ship a container, it would call an API, the latency
graph would look strange, and I'd be the one asked why. And I could answer the infrastructure half of
the question — the pod is throttled, the connection pool is exhausted, the retry storm is your own
fault — but not the other half. Why does the same request cost 400ms sometimes and 4 seconds other
times? Why did quality "get worse" after a deploy that changed no code?

I could keep answering half the question for the next five years. That seemed like a bad plan.

## The honest version

There's a tidier story I could tell here about strategic career positioning. The real reason is
simpler: I got tired of being the person in the room who understood the deployment but not the thing
being deployed.

<Callout type="note" label="Starting position">
  I can run systems. I can make them observable, reproducible and recoverable. I cannot yet reason
  about what happens *inside* the box, and that gap is starting to matter in my day job.
</Callout>

## What I'm actually betting on

My bet is that the infrastructure part of ML is closer to my existing skills than it looks, and that
the modelling part is further away than I'd like to admit. Some early guesses:

- A model registry is an artifact repository with extra opinions about lineage.
- A feature store is a cache with a consistency problem and better marketing.
- A training run is a batch job that lies about its resource usage.
- Evaluation is the part I will underestimate. (Writing this down so I can be wrong in public.)

If those guesses hold, the migration is mostly vocabulary and a handful of genuinely new concepts. If
they don't, that's more interesting and I'll write that up instead.

## The rules for this journal

I'm going to try to keep this useful rather than impressive:

1. Write the entry after the thing works, not after I've read about the thing working.
2. Include what broke. The broken part is the only part that proves I actually ran it.
3. No conclusions I can't defend. "I don't know yet" is an acceptable ending.
4. Keep it short enough that I actually publish it.
